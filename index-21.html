Cal Poly Pomona  ·  Mechatronics Laboratory Project Self-Balancing Platform (Thrust Vector Control Model) Fall 2024  ·  Team of 3

Built a two-axis self-balancing platform that holds level against disturbances, modeling how thrust vector control keeps a rocket pointed true, using an IMU, a complementary filter, and PID control to keep angle error within 2 degrees.

Control SystemsSensor FusionArduinoC++3D Printing
Read ReportView Code

Project Detail

  • Built an autonomous platform on a 3D-printed pivoting frame designed in CAD. An MPU6050 accelerometer and gyroscope measure tilt, and two servo motors correct it in real time, controlled by an Arduino Uno.
  • Calibrated the sensor from stationary readings, then fused the gyroscope's short-term accuracy with the accelerometer's long-term stability using a complementary filter to get clean roll and pitch angles.
  • Tuned a PID controller over repeated tests to balance stability against responsiveness. The platform recovered quickly from tilts with minimal oscillation and held its angle error within 2 degrees.
  • Worked through sensor noise and servo jitter by calibrating the sensor, tuning the filter, and stabilizing the power supply. A stepper-driven propeller to add a thrust disturbance was designed but not integrated in time.
  • Next steps: a Kalman filter to reduce noise, a third degree of freedom, smoother actuation with stepper motors, and a microcontroller with more I/O.
  • The Arduino code is on GitHub, with a list of its known limitations, including gyroscope drift that the current filter does not fully correct. View the code →

Cal Poly Pomona  ·  Measurements Final Project Eddy Current Tachometer Fall 2025  ·  Team of 3

Built a working non-contact tachometer that measures motor speed from eddy-current drag, with both an analog pointer and a digital LCD readout, for a total parts cost of $62.49.

InstrumentationArduinoSensorsCalibration3D Printing
View Slides

Project Detail

  • Built a working non-contact tachometer with a team of 3. A magnet spinning near an aluminum drag cup induces eddy currents (Faraday and Lenz), and the drag torque deflects a spring-loaded pointer in proportion to speed, with an Arduino and AS5600 encoder driving a digital readout.
  • Found that the magnet-to-cup spacing (3 to 5 mm) strongly affects the reading, and calibrated the pointer against a reference laser tachometer at speeds up to about 8,100 RPM.
  • Built for $62.49 using 3D-printed parts. Next steps: more calibration points, a refined drag-cup design, and smoother signal processing.

Graduate CourseworkANSYS FluentCFDHeat TransferThermal Energy StorageEnergy Systems
View Slides

Project Detail

  • Modeled a 0.25 m by 0.25 m by 1 m solid storage block with a central air tube and an embedded electric heater, using a half model with symmetry planes, a three-mesh grid-refinement study, and a k-omega turbulence model for the discharge.
  • Computed energy stored (about 13.6 MJ during charge) and removed (about 0.29 MJ during discharge) from storage temperature, air exit temperature, and wall heat flux results.
  • Traced the low round-trip efficiency (about 2 percent) to the short discharge, low storage conductivity, and high air flow rate, and proposed multiple tubes, a higher-conductivity material, and a longer discharge.

NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory  ·  Senior Project Report

WISTLS Sampling Drill: Design and Development

Jan 2025 – Dec 2025  ·  Cal Poly Pomona  ·  Faculty Advisor: Prof. Behnam Bahr

A compact auger drill for lunar regolith sampling, scoped by NASA JPL for the WISTLS instrument and built around a planned 2027 lunar rover mission. The report covers requirements, design, analytical validation, and a planned experimental test program.

NASA JPLSolidWorksISRUStructural AnalysisMaterial Selection
Read Report Design Slides Test Plan Slides

Overview

  • Scope and requirements came from NASA JPL's WISTLS (Water Isotope Tunable Laser Spectrometer) mission, working with JPL engineers Kalind Carpenter, Lance Christensen, and Kevin Schwarm. Designed a compact auger drill to collect at least 20 g of regolith under cryogenic temperatures, minimal power, and tight mass and volume limits.
  • Final design: 30 mm cutting bit, helical auger flights, 5 mm Ti-6Al-4V drive shaft, and a protective sheath. Total mass 36.3 g, with an estimated 22.7 g sample yield (13.5% over the requirement).
  • Found that the original aluminum shaft would yield (factor of safety 0.40) and redesigned it in titanium with a larger diameter, reaching a factor of safety of 2.70.
  • Validation so far is analytical (hand calculations). Developed a nine-condition test plan (particle size by moisture content) to measure cutting torque, auger uptake efficiency, and sample retention. View the test plan slides →
  • Optimized for metal additive manufacturing, with ongoing collaboration with JPL to fabricate and physically test the drill.
